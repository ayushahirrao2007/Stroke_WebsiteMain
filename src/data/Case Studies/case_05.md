# Learning Points

1. Watershed infarctions result from global cerebral hypoperfusion, not necessarily arterial occlusion.
2. Profound hypotension is the most important clinical clue.
3. **Man-in-the-Barrel Syndrome** (predominant bilateral arm weakness) is a classic presentation of ACA–MCA watershed infarction.
4. A normal CT angiogram does not exclude ischemic stroke in hypoperfusion syndromes.
5. CT Perfusion and MRI DWI are particularly valuable for confirming watershed infarctions.

# ① Intravenous Fluid Resuscitation

## Fluid Administered

**0.9% Normal Saline**

## Initial Dose

**500–1000 mL bolus**, followed by reassessment and further isotonic fluids based on blood pressure, urine output, and cardiac status.

## Why was Normal Saline chosen?

- Corrects hypovolaemia.
- Restores cerebral perfusion pressure.
- Improves systemic blood pressure.
- Prevents progression of watershed ischemia.
- Does not increase cerebral oedema.

## Why not Dextrose?

Routine dextrose-containing fluids are avoided unless hypoglycaemia is present because hyperglycaemia worsens ischemic brain injury.

---

# Clinical Pearls

1. Watershed infarctions are caused by cerebral hypoperfusion rather than major arterial occlusion.
2. Severe hypotension and dehydration are key precipitating factors.
3. **"Man-in-the-Barrel Syndrome"**—bilateral arm weakness with relative leg sparing—is a classic presentation of ACA–MCA watershed infarction.
4. A normal CT angiogram does not exclude ischemic stroke in hypoperfusion syndromes.
5. CT Perfusion and MRI DWI are highly valuable for identifying border-zone infarctions.
6. The first therapeutic priority is restoration of systemic perfusion, not simply antithrombotic therapy.
7. Correction of electrolyte abnormalities and treatment of the underlying illness are essential to prevent progression and recurrence.
8. Homoeopathic care, when used, should be individualized and integrated only after the patient is medically stabilized.
