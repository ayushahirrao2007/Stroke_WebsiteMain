# Key Learning Points

1. SCA infarction typically presents with ipsilateral cerebellar signs, including **dysmetria, intention tremor, dysarthria, and severe gait ataxia**, while limb strength is usually preserved.
2. A normal CT scan does not exclude posterior circulation ischemic stroke; **MRI-DWI** remains the most sensitive imaging modality.
3. CT angiography is essential for identifying **SCA or basilar artery occlusion** and planning reperfusion therapy.
4. Cerebellar strokes often have low NIHSS scores despite causing profound functional disability due to severe imbalance and incoordination.
5. Large cerebellar infarctions require close monitoring because oedema may lead to brainstem compression or obstructive hydrocephalus.
6. Early recognition and prompt reperfusion therapy, when indicated, significantly improve neurological and functional outcomes.

# Possible Remedy Considerations

Depending on the individualized presentation:

- **Gelsemium sempervirens** – if the patient's totality includes dizziness, muscular weakness, heaviness, and sluggishness.
- **Cocculus indicus** – when vertigo, nausea, imbalance, and motion intolerance predominate in the individualized symptom picture.
- **Conium maculatum** – when vertigo is aggravated by head movement and matches the patient's constitutional profile.
- **Causticum** – during rehabilitation for persistent neurological weakness when constitutionally indicated.
- **Arnica montana** – if the overall symptom picture is compatible with the remedy following vascular injury.

### Reason for Selection

Remedies are selected based on individualized classical homoeopathic principles and not solely on the diagnosis of SCA infarction.

# Clinical Pearls

1. Severe truncal ataxia with preserved muscle strength strongly suggests **cerebellar stroke** rather than peripheral vertigo.
2. SCA infarction commonly causes **dysmetria, intention tremor, dysarthria, rebound phenomenon, and ipsilateral gait ataxia**.
3. Early CT may be normal; **MRI-DWI** is the most sensitive investigation for acute cerebellar infarction.
4. Large cerebellar infarcts require close observation because oedema can cause **brainstem compression or obstructive hydrocephalus**.
5. Low NIHSS scores do not exclude severe disability in posterior circulation strokes.
6. Early thrombolysis, when indicated, followed by coordinated multidisciplinary rehabilitation significantly improves long-term functional recovery.
7. Vestibular rehabilitation is a key component of recovery after cerebellar infarction.
