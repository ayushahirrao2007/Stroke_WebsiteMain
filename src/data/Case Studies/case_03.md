# Learning Points

1. ACA strokes classically produce contralateral leg weakness greater than arm weakness.
2. Behavioural changes such as abulia are characteristic of medial frontal lobe involvement.
3. Preservation of speech and facial strength helps differentiate ACA strokes from MCA strokes.
4. CT angiography is valuable for confirming ACA occlusion and defining the vascular territory.
5. MRI with DWI and ADC sequences is highly sensitive for detecting early ischemic infarction.

# Homoeopathic Perspective (After Medical Stabilization)

Homoeopathic treatment should only be considered after the patient has been medically stabilized and should never replace emergency stroke management.

## Individualized Case-Taking Includes

- Mental generals
- Physical generals
- Constitutional characteristics
- Past history
- Family history
- Miasmatic evaluation
- Totality of symptoms

## Possible Remedy Considerations

Depending on the complete symptom picture, a homoeopathic physician may consider:

- **Causticum** – when paralysis and motor weakness match the remedy profile.
- **Gelsemium** – when weakness, heaviness, and sluggishness predominate.
- **Baryta carbonica** – in selected elderly patients with constitutional indications.
- **Aurum metallicum** – where depressive features and constitutional characteristics correspond.
- **Arnica montana** – if indicated by the overall symptom totality during recovery.

**Reason for Selection:** Remedies are prescribed according to individualized homoeopathic principles rather than the diagnosis alone.

# Clinical Pearls

1. ACA strokes characteristically produce contralateral leg weakness greater than arm weakness.
2. Abulia is a key frontal lobe sign and should not be mistaken for aphasia or depression.
3. Preservation of facial strength and upper limb power helps distinguish ACA strokes from MCA strokes.
4. CTA is valuable in identifying distal ACA occlusions that may be difficult to appreciate clinically.
5. MRI confirms early ischemic changes in the medial frontal cortex and paracentral lobule.
6. Early mobilization and gait rehabilitation are central to recovery because lower limb weakness is the predominant deficit.
7. Risk factor modification—including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, and obesity—is essential to prevent recurrent stroke.
8. Homoeopathic management, when used, should complement rehabilitation after medical stabilization and should never delay evidence-based acute stroke treatment.
